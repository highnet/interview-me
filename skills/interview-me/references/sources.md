# Sources

This skill's method is built from five books on interviewing, chosen because each one covers a different part of drawing out what someone actually means. The agent is the interviewer. The user is describing their own idea, not being sold to. So every technique below has been adapted for understanding, never for persuasion.

**About the quotes.** Quotes marked ✓ were checked against the publisher, the author's own words, or several independent sources. Anything we couldn't verify word for word is written as a paraphrase and attributed by idea, not in quotation marks. No page numbers are given because we haven't checked them against print editions. Corrections are welcome by pull request.

---

## 1. The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick)

Fitzpatrick, Rob. *The Mom Test: How to Talk to Customers & Learn If Your Business Is a Good Idea When Everyone Is Lying to You.* CreateSpace, 2013.

> "You want facts and commitments, not compliments." ✓

> "Opinions are worthless." ✓ (shortened. The full line exempts experts who have built something very similar.)

**What the skill takes from it**
- **Past specifics over hypotheticals.** Ask "When did this last get in your way? What did you do?" rather than "Would you want X?"
- **Treat "always", "usually", and "would" as warning signs.** Pin each one to a real instance.
- **Dig under feature requests.** "It should have X" → "What would X let you do that you can't do now?"
- **Ask about today's workaround.** It shows how much the user really cares and what constraints they're under.
- **Don't pitch.** The agent doesn't sell its own ideas in the middle of the interview.

## 2. Interviewing Users (Steve Portigal)

Portigal, Steve. *Interviewing Users: How to Uncover Compelling Insights.* 2nd ed. Rosenfeld Media, 2023. ISBN 978-1-959029-78-6.

Paraphrase: silence after a question is a tool, not a failure. Interviewers often rush to suggest answers when a pause makes them uncomfortable, and that's a mistake.

**What the skill takes from it**
- **Leave room.** In chat, "Say more about that" often beats a new question.
- **Use their words.** Repeat the user's own terms back, not the agent's jargon.
- **Most of the work is in the follow-ups.** Ask for an example, the order things happen in, and the exception.
- **One neutral, open question at a time.** No multi-part questions, no leading ones, no yes/no framing.
- **Start broad, then narrow.** Begin with context and goals, then move to details.

## 3. Exploring Requirements (Gause & Weinberg)

Gause, Donald C., and Gerald M. Weinberg. *Exploring Requirements: Quality Before Design.* Dorset House, 1989. ISBN 978-0-932633-13-2.

The book's best-known contribution is its set of **context-free questions**: high-level questions that fit almost any project. They're asked before anyone talks about solutions.

**What the skill takes from it**
- **Context-free questions first:** who it's for, what problem it solves, what a great outcome looks like, what environment it lives in, and what solving it is worth.
- **Meta-questions** about the interview itself: "Am I asking the right things? What should I be asking that I'm not?"
- **Point out ambiguity.** When a phrase has two readings, show both and let the user pick. Never choose one silently.
- **Sort what you hear** into functions (what it does), attributes (how it should feel), constraints (what can't move), and preferences (what would be nice). Then confirm the sorting with the user.

## 4. The Coaching Habit (Michael Bungay Stanier)

Bungay Stanier, Michael. *The Coaching Habit: Say Less, Ask More & Change the Way You Lead Forever.* Box of Crayons Press, 2016.

> "And what else?" ✓ (the book's "AWE question")

> "What's the real challenge here for you?" ✓ (the Focus question)

Paraphrase of the book's thesis, in the author's words from interviews: stay curious a little longer, and rush to advice and action a little more slowly.

**What the skill takes from it**
- **Open with "What's on your mind?"** and let the user set the shape of the idea.
- **"And what else?"** Ask it again until nothing new comes up, then stop.
- **"What's the real challenge here for you?"** cuts from the feature they asked for to the problem underneath.
- **"If you're saying yes to this, what are you saying no to?"** brings out scope and trade-offs.
- **Hold back the urge to give advice.** No solutions until the brief is confirmed.

## 5. Never Split the Difference (Chris Voss)

Voss, Chris, with Tahl Raz. *Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It.* Harper Business, 2016.

Paraphrase: you've reached real alignment when the other person says "that's right", which is different from "you're right".

**What the skill takes from it**
- **Mirroring.** Repeat the last few key words as a question ("…for the whole team?") and wait.
- **Labeling.** Name what seems to matter: "It sounds like speed matters more than polish here." The user confirms or corrects it.
- **Calibrated questions.** Open "what" and "how" questions: "What makes this hard today?" "How will you know it worked?"
- **No-oriented questions.** "Would it be a mistake to leave X out of the first version?" These are easier to answer honestly than questions fishing for a yes.
- **Hunt for black swans.** Look for the unknown fact that would change everything: "Is there anything about this I'd never guess?"
- **Play it back until you hear "that's right".** That's when the interview is done.

## Considered, not chosen

- **Humble Inquiry** (Edgar & Peter Schein, Berrett-Koehler, 2nd ed. 2021) describes itself as "the gentle art of drawing someone out" ✓. It's the source of the skill's stance of not-knowing, but it's light on concrete techniques.
- **Competing Against Luck** (Christensen et al., 2016) is where the "what progress are you trying to make?" framing comes from.
- **Continuous Discovery Habits** (Torres), **Just Enough Research** (Hall), **Practical Empathy** (Young). All excellent, but they mostly overlap with the five above or focus on research programmes rather than single conversations.
